# LRD Stabilizer: Experiment Log & Narrative

**Project:** Layer-Wise Representation Divergence Stabilizer for Phi-3.5-mini-instruct  
**Models:** Phi-3.5-mini-instruct (primary), Llama-3 8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen2.5-7B  
**Task:** GSM8K (primary), MMLU, BBH  
**Goal:** Train a lightweight, inference-time stabilizer that corrects perturbation-induced hidden state divergence without requiring a clean reference pass at inference.

---

## Background: What the Diagnostics Established

Before any stabilizer training, a systematic diagnostic study established four empirical claims about how perturbations damage model representations. These findings directly motivated every architectural decision in the stabilizer design — and their implications were not fully understood until several training failures made them concrete.

### The LRD Framework

Layer-wise Representation Divergence (LRD) at layer L is defined as:

```
LRD(L) = 1 − cosine_similarity(h̄_clean^(L), h̄_noisy^(L))
```

where h̄^(L) is the mean-pooled hidden state. A value of 0 means identical representations; higher values indicate greater divergence.

### Key Diagnostic Findings

**Claim 1 — Perturbations cause measurable latent divergence.** Confirmed across all tested models, but the *propagation pattern* is architecture-dependent:
- **Phi-3.5-mini**: Spike-and-suppress. Peak divergence at layer 0 (embedding), rapid collapse to near-zero by layer 2–3, small residual persisting to the final layer.
- **Mistral-7B, Llama-3 8B, Qwen2.5-7B**: Late-accumulation. Near-zero LRD at early layers, progressive rise from mid-network to the final layer.

**Claim 2 — Divergence is not corrected by self-attention.** Transformer self-attention trained on clean data is insufficient to repair perturbation-induced divergence regardless of propagation regime. Phi-3.5 passively fails to recover; Mistral actively amplifies divergence.

**Claim 3 — Final-layer LRD predicts task failure.** Significant by Mann-Whitney U test for directional perturbation types (typos, OCR, speech, homophones) across both confirmed models and all three datasets. Uniform perturbations (case, whitespace) produce high absolute LRD but are non-predictive of failure — a critical distinction for stabilizer training.

**Claim 4 — Failure is causally localised (activation patching).** Patching clean hidden states into the noisy forward pass, one layer at a time, reveals where perturbation effects are encoded:

| Model | LRD Regime | Causal Window | Peak Recovery | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phi-3.5-mini | Spike-and-suppress | Layers 1–8 | ~85% | Confirmed |
| Llama-3 8B | Late-accumulation | Layers 0–8 | ~67% | Confirmed |
| Mistral-7B | Late-accumulation | Layers 1–8 est. | 65% ceiling | Exploratory |
| Qwen2.5-7B | Late-accumulation | Layers 1–12 | 68.4% at layer 12 | Exploratory |

**Critical finding:** LRD propagation regime and causal encoding window are *dissociable*. Llama-3 and Mistral share Qwen's late-accumulation pattern but have early causal windows consistent with Phi-3.5. The causal window is an architecture-dependent property that cannot be inferred from the LRD curve alone.

### Perturbation Taxonomy

Two-class taxonomy confirmed cross-model via MW test:

| Class | Types | LRD Pattern | Predicts Failure? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Directional | Typos, OCR, Speech, Homophones | Moderate LRD, wrong-group higher | Yes |
| Uniform | Case, Whitespace | High absolute LRD, similar in both groups | No |

> **Narrative point:** The taxonomy finding is important for stabilizer design. A stabilizer that minimises LRD indiscriminately would expend capacity correcting uniform perturbations that don't cause task failure. The diagnostic section motivates training the stabilizer to track *failure-predictive* divergence, not raw LRD magnitude.

## Stabilizer Design: Initial Architecture

Based on the diagnostic findings, the initial stabilizer design targeted Phi-3.5 with insertion at layers 1–8 (the confirmed causal window):

**Stabilizer network:** Two-layer MLP with bottleneck ratio r=4 (d=3072 → 768 → 3072). Outputs a correction vector.

**Controller head:** Small MLP outputting scalar gate α ∈ [0,1] via sigmoid. Controls magnitude of correction.

**Forward pass per insertion point:**
```
correction = stabilizer(h_noisy)
alpha = controller(h_noisy)
h_out = h_noisy + alpha * correction
```

**Loss:**
```
L = λ·L_stab + (1−λ)·L_acc
```

where L_stab is MSE between h_corrected(L) and h_clean(L) at each insertion layer.

**Design rationale:** The gate and stabilizer both read h_noisy directly — no clean reference needed at inference. Single forward pass. Freeze base model weights; only train stabilizer and controller parameters (~150M added parameters, ~9% of model).

## V1: Initial Training — Gate Control Failure

**Result:**

| Condition | No Stabilizer | With Stabilizer | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clean baseline | 79% | 62% | −17% |
| Typos 5% | 63% | 50% | −13% |
| OCR 5% | 59% | 47% | −12% |
| Whitespace 10% | 60% | 57% | −3% |
| Case 10% | 71% | 58% | −13% |
| Speech 10% | 76% | 66% | −10% |
| Homophones 20% | 72% | 62% | −10% |

**Diagnosis:** Three independent failures:

1. **Gate never learned to stay closed on clean inputs.** gate_mean stayed low (~0.04) but gate_max grew continuously to ~0.45. A small number of layers fired at ~50% activation on every input, clean or noisy.

2. **Gate suppression term was broken by design.** The suppression penalty tracked gate_mean, which was already below threshold. The actual problem was gate_max — a handful of high-gate layers went unpenalised.

3. **λ_gate = 0.001 was too weak.** The sparsity penalty was 1/500th the weight of the stabilization loss. Gradient pressure from L_stab overwhelmed the signal keeping gates closed.

**Changes for v2:** Switch gate suppression from L1-mean to L_inf-max, increase lambda_gate substantially (0.001 → 0.01–0.1), add hard gate cap at inference.

> **Narrative point:** The v1 failure established a pattern that would recur: the gate cannot reliably distinguish clean from perturbed inputs when reading mid-network hidden states. This was initially attributed to training hyperparameters, but the real cause — that Phi-3.5's spike-and-suppress pattern collapses the divergence signal before it reaches the gate — would only become clear after several more runs.

## Gate Ablation & Propagation Test: Correction Direction Is Wrong

Before retraining, two diagnostic experiments were run to separate gate failure from correction direction failure.

**Gate ablation (gate=1 always):**

| Condition | No Stab | Learned Gate | Gate=1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clean baseline | 79% | 62% | 1% |
| Typos 5% | 58% | 49% | 1% |
| OCR 5% | 57% | 44% | 1% |

**Finding:** Gate=1 collapses accuracy to ~1% universally. The correction MLP learned transformations that are catastrophically destructive when applied at full strength. The gate at ~45% activation was the only thing preventing total failure — it was acting as damage control rather than modulation.

**Propagation-aware training:**

Added probe layers at 12, 18, and 31 to provide downstream correction signal. Result: −55% on clean baseline, gate snapped fully open by step 1500.

Two compounding failures:
1. Gate drove to gate_max ≈ 1.0 within 1500 steps, applying full destructive corrections.
2. Propagation gradients vanished. gnorm = 0.000 for nearly every logged step — the gradient path from probe layers 12, 18, 31 back through ~20 frozen transformer blocks to the stabilizer at layers 1–8 attenuated to effectively zero.

> **Narrative point:** The propagation experiment produced the clearest mechanistic insight of the project: the gradient path through frozen transformer blocks is too long to carry useful signal. This directly motivated the later decision to place probe layers immediately after the intervention zone (layer 9 only), and ultimately to move the primary intervention to the embedding layer where probe distance is minimal.

**Diagnosis:** The free MLP optimised toward hidden-state alignment in a direction orthogonal to task performance. The correction direction is fundamentally wrong — not a magnitude problem tunable through hyperparameters.

## V2/V3: LoRA Adapter + Architecture Fixes

**Changes:**
- Replaced free MLP with LoRA-style low-rank adapter (rank 64, later reduced to 16): `h' = h + gate * (B @ A @ h)`
- Standard LoRA init (B=0 at init, so adapter starts as perfect no-op)
- Fixed teacher-forcing shortcut: the clean answer concatenated into noisy_full_ids gave the adapter free access to answer-position hidden states, allowing CE minimization by injecting answer-correlated information without learning to denoise. Fixed by masking stabilizer hooks to prompt positions only.
- Fixed gate suppression to use max-based penalty
- Added clean-input adapter norm penalty
- Rebalanced loss weights: regularization terms elevated to be competitive with reconstruction terms

**v3 result (after shortcut fix):**

| Condition | Δ |
|---|---|
| Clean baseline | −7% |
| Speech 10% | **+4%** ← first positive result |
| OCR 5% | 0% (neutral) |
| Whitespace 10% | −12% |
| Typos 5% | −4% |

**Significance:** The first positive result. The architecture was doing something real. The teacher-forcing shortcut fix was decisive — L_acc no longer collapsed to 0.07 during training, confirming the shortcut was closed.

**Remaining problems:**
- Gate too conservative: gate_mean ≈ 0.0002, gate_max ≈ 0.037. Curriculum's regularization kept the gate nearly shut the entire run, so only tiny corrections were being applied.
- Whitespace regression at −12% suggested corrections were being applied to inputs that don't need them.

> **Narrative point:** The teacher-forcing shortcut is an important methodological finding. When the stabilizer has access to answer tokens during training, it can satisfy both L_stab and L_acc by injecting answer-correlated information into prompt-position hidden states — a solution that works during training but fails catastrophically at inference when no answer context is present. This shortcut is subtle enough that it would have been easy to miss without careful diagnostic logging, and it illustrates a general risk in training interventions on autoregressive models with teacher forcing.

## V4: Extended Training + Whitespace Downweighting

**Changes:**
- Extended run from 15k → 25k steps to let curriculum decay reach genuinely below 1.0×
- Downweighted whitespace examples in L_stab and L_dir (0.3× weight) — whitespace is a uniform perturbation type that is non-predictive of failure; including it at full weight was biasing the correction subspace

**Result vs v3:**

| Condition | v3 Δ | v4 Δ |
|---|---|---|
| Clean baseline | −7% | −2% |
| Typos | −4% | **+6%** |
| OCR | 0% | −8% |
| Whitespace | −12% | **−2%** |
| Case | −4% | −6% |
| Speech | +4% | +6% |
| Homophones | −4% | −9% |

**Finding:** Whitespace improved dramatically (−12% → −2%), confirming the downweighting worked. Typos flipped positive. But OCR and Homophones got worse despite being in the same directional perturbation class as Typos and Speech.

**Gate still essentially closed:** gate_max ≈ 0.040 at step 25k, nearly identical to v3's 0.037. The extra 10k steps didn't open it further.

> **Narrative point:** The divergence between Typos (+6%) and OCR (−8%) within the same perturbation taxonomy class is an important finding. It suggests the correction subspace is not capturing a single "directional perturbation correction direction" — it's capturing something more specific to the geometry of word-level versus character-level displacements. This foreshadows the core correction direction problem that becomes the central challenge from v5 onward.

## V5: MLP Replacement — Correction Mechanism Validated

**Motivation:** LoRA constrains correction to a low-rank linear subspace. Character-substitution perturbations (Typos, OCR) produce hidden state displacements that may not be well-approximated by a linear transformation. The LoRA constraint may be underpowered for these cases.

**Changes:**
- Replaced LoRA adapter with norm-clipped bottleneck MLP:
```python
h_down = gelu(W_down @ h)      # d → bottleneck (64)
h_up = W_up @ h_down           # bottleneck → d
delta = h_up / (h_up.norm() + eps) * max_norm
h_out = h + gate * delta
```
- Accelerated curriculum: lambda_reg_start 2.0, stab_warmup_frac 0.15

**Result vs v4:**

| Condition | v4 Δ | v5 Δ |
|---|---|---|
| Clean baseline | −2% | **−1%** |
| Typos | +6% | −2% |
| OCR | −8% | −8% |
| Whitespace | −2% | **+8%** |
| Case | −6% | **+4%** |
| Speech | +6% | +4% |
| Homophones | −9% | **+1%** |

**Gate finally opened:** gate_max ≈ 0.30 vs v4's 0.04. The bottleneck MLP gives the gate a more useful gradient signal.

**Key finding:** Whitespace, Case, and Homophones all flipped positive. The LoRA correction was underpowered — confirmed. However OCR remained at −8% in both v4 and v5 despite the architectural change, which definitively places the OCR problem in training signal or gate behavior, not correction mechanism capacity.

> **Narrative point:** The LoRA vs MLP comparison is a clean controlled experiment — same training setup, same fixes, different correction mechanism. The results make a concrete empirical argument for nonlinear correction mechanisms in stabilizer design, with direct connection to the geometry of different perturbation types.

## V6 Gate Diagnosis: The Fundamental Limitation

**Gate analysis (per-condition stats, late training):**

| Condition | gate_mean |
|---|---|
| Clean | 0.01144 |
| Typos | 0.01253 |
| OCR | 0.01239 |
| Speech | 0.01267 |
| Homophones | 0.01186 |
| Whitespace | 0.01313 |
| Case | 0.01403 |

**Noisy/clean gate ratio: 1.048** — essentially identical to v5's 1.004 despite adding LRD supervision (λ_lrd=0.40) and EMA-based gate input augmentation.

**Finding:** gate_max ≈ 0.31 but gate_mean ≈ 0.01. The gate fires on a small fraction of token positions that are structurally similar regardless of perturbation type — likely special tokens or positional patterns. The EMA divergence augmentation was supposed to signal perturbation-induced shifts, but the EMA absorbs the variation rather than signalling it.

**The gate is not the lever.** The MLP is applying a near-constant correction direction to everything. Speech and whitespace benefit from that direction; Typos and OCR are hurt by it. No gate fix will resolve this as long as the gate reads mid-network hidden states where Phi-3.5's spike-and-suppress has already collapsed the divergence signal.

**v6 GSM8K-only results:**

| Condition | Δ |
|---|---|
| Clean baseline | **0.0%** ← perfectly neutral |
| Speech | +5% |
| Whitespace | +4% |
| Typos | **−9%** ← getting worse |
| OCR | −2% |
| Case | −1% |
| Homophones | −3% |

**Typos confirmed real regression at n=200:** −4.5%, worse than n=100 result. OCR confirmed at −6%.

> **Narrative point:** Six runs of consistent evidence converge on the same conclusion: the gate cannot learn to discriminate perturbation from clean when reading mid-network hidden states on Phi-3.5. This is not a hyperparameter problem — it reflects a fundamental property of the model's spike-and-suppress pattern. The divergence has been suppressed before it reaches the gate. This negative result is as informative as the positive ones: it establishes that mid-network gating is the wrong design for spike-and-suppress architectures.

## Root Cause Analysis: The Spike-and-Suppress Tension

The accumulated evidence across six runs reveals a single underlying tension that was present from the start but only became fully visible through the failures:

**The causal window and the signal-rich zone are different parts of the network.**

Activation patching establishes that intervening at layers 1–8 is causally effective — correcting hidden states there does recover task performance. But Phi-3.5's spike-and-suppress pattern collapses LRD from its peak at layer 0 to near-zero by layer 3. By the time the stabilizer modules are reading h_noisy at layers 1–8, the divergence signal has already been largely suppressed by the model's own attention mechanism. The gate is reading a signal that genuinely does not vary much between clean and noisy inputs at those layers.

This tension has two consequences:
1. The gate cannot discriminate — confirmed across six runs
2. The correction direction is dominated by perturbation types with larger residual displacements at layers 1–8 (whitespace, speech), drowning out character-substitution types (typos, OCR) whose displacements are smallest after suppression

**The implication:** The primary correction should happen at the embedding layer (layer 0), where LRD is at its peak and the perturbation signal is maximally present, *before* the transformer's suppression mechanism acts on it. The patching results show early intervention is causally effective — layer 0 is earlier than layers 1–8, and represents the point where the perturbation is most detectable.

## V7: Two-Stage Embedding-Level Architecture (Current)

**Architectural redesign motivated by root cause analysis:**

### Stage 1 — Primary correction at embedding layer
- Insert before layer 0, operating on token embeddings directly
- Gate reads raw token embeddings: character-level perturbations are directly visible as embedding distance here
- Gate input: `concat([emb, |emb - emb_clean_mean|])` — the EMA deviation is meaningful at the embedding level in a way it was not at mid-network layers
- Norm-clipped bottleneck MLP (bottleneck=64)
- **Contrastive objective** rather than MSE reconstruction:
```
L_contrastive = max(0, d(h_corrected, h_clean) - d(h_noisy, h_clean) + margin)
```
  where d is cosine distance and margin ≈ 0.1. This pushes the corrected embedding closer to h_clean than h_noisy is, without requiring exact match. Naturally handles non-failure-predictive perturbations: if h_noisy is already close to h_clean (case, whitespace), loss is near zero.

### Stage 2 — Refinement at layers 2 and 4 only
- Light correction modules, smaller bottleneck (32)
- Standard MSE reconstruction against h_clean at those layers
- Expected to stay mostly closed — safety valve for residual divergence surviving suppression
- Probe layer at layer 9 only (1 frozen block gap, avoids vanishing gradients)

### Loss function
```
L_total = λ_contrast · L_contrastive      # stage 1, noisy steps
        + λ_stab · L_stab                 # stage 2 MSE, layers 2+4
        + λ_prop · L_prop                 # probe at layer 9
        + λ_acc · L_acc                   # task CE (independent floor)
        + λ_gate · L_gate_max             # max-based sparsity
        + λ_suppress · L_suppress         # clean step gate suppression
        + λ_adapter · L_adapter_clean     # adapter norm on clean steps
```

### Training data
Multidomain mix: 40% GSM8K, 30% MMLU, 30% C4 general web. Same perturbation augmentation applied to all sources.

**Success criterion at step 3000:** gate_ratio_stage1 > 1.3. In all previous runs, gate failure was detectable by step 3000. If the embedding-level gate cannot discriminate at step 3000, the architectural assumption is wrong and the design requires further revision.

## Narrative Points for Paper

**1. The diagnostic section motivates the stabilizer design.**
The LRD framework, propagation regimes, causal windows, and perturbation taxonomy are not just empirical findings — they are design constraints. Every architectural decision in the stabilizer has a direct diagnostic motivation. This is novel relative to prior robustness work, which builds correction mechanisms heuristically.

**2. The negative results are as informative as the positive ones.**
The failure progression from v1 to v6 is not a series of mistakes — it is a systematic elimination of candidate explanations. Each failure mode was diagnosable using the same LRD framework that motivated the design. The gate discrimination failure, the correction direction bias, the gradient vanishing through frozen layers, and the teacher-forcing shortcut are all mechanistically explained. This is stronger than a paper that presents only a working system.

**3. The spike-and-suppress tension is a general finding.**
The insight that causal window and signal-rich zone are different parts of the network for spike-and-suppress architectures generalises beyond Phi-3.5. Any stabilizer targeting a model with this propagation pattern faces the same tension. The two-stage architecture that intervenes at the embedding before suppression and refines within the causal window is a principled response to this general challenge.

**4. The architecture-dependent causal window finding adds a cross-model dimension.**
Qwen's broader causal window (layers 1–12 versus layers 1–8 for other models) despite sharing the late-accumulation LRD regime with Mistral and Llama-3 demonstrates that LRD propagation pattern and causal encoding window are dissociable. This means a single stabilizer insertion range cannot be optimal across architectures — either architecture-specific configs or a dynamic controller that learns its own intervention depth is needed for generalisation.

**5. The perturbation taxonomy has practical implications.**
The directional/uniform taxonomy confirmed in the diagnostic section directly informs stabilizer training: uniform perturbations (case, whitespace) should be downweighted in the reconstruction loss because they produce high LRD that does not predict failure. Training against them at full weight biases the correction subspace toward non-failure-predictive geometry. The v4 whitespace downweighting result (−12% → −2%) is a direct empirical confirmation of this.

**6. The contrastive objective is motivated by the taxonomy.**
The switch from MSE reconstruction to a contrastive objective at the embedding level is not arbitrary — it follows directly from the finding that high LRD does not always predict failure. A contrastive objective that requires the corrected representation to be *closer* to h_clean than h_noisy, without requiring exact match, naturally produces near-zero loss for non-failure-predictive perturbations where h_noisy is already close enough. This is tighter alignment between the diagnostic framework and the training objective than MSE provides.
